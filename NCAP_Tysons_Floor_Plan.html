What I Read From the Photos (and What I'm Inferring)

  • Six lanes, not eight. The starting-block row shows six red Speedo blocks. Eight-lane pools are 60 ft wide — too wide for what the photos show relative to the deck and roof structure. 42 ft water width is consistent.
  • Starting blocks mark the deep end. NFHS allows starts in water as shallow as 4 ft with approved blocks, but the usual training-pool geometry deepens under the blocks to 6–8 ft. Your 8'-6" deep end fits the pattern, and the block-end photo shows no "NO DIVING" graphic on that wall.
  • The "4" tiles are on the shallow end. Those red-square depth markers are always on the shallow wall/gutter line. The same frame shows the "NO DIVING" text — consistent.
  • Hopper, not a true diving well. A pure diving well is 12+ ft. 8'-6" with a short steep transition says "safe for starts, not for diving boards." No springboard or platform is visible in any frame.
  • The ramp is at the shallow end corner. Photo with the handrail and tile step is the accessible entry (ADA Title III requires an entry means; this pool has a ramp rather than a lift, which is less common).
  • Ceiling is a gable with a raised monitor. You can see clerestory-like panels at the high point in two of the long-view frames, and the bar-joist pattern steps up at mid-span. Monitors are popular for daylighting but they're a cold surface in winter — classic spot for condensation drip over lanes.
  • Supply is overhead (legacy layout). Round spiral duct visible along the ridge, with lateral drops. This is the older natatorium HVAC approach — it's what the handbook Ch. 4 describes as "dilution ventilation." The downside you'd feel in this specific room: chloramine air tends to settle in a 4–6 ft thick layer over the water surface where swimmers breathe, because overhead supply doesn't push it down to the gutter for source capture.
  • Fogging visible in several frames. The haze around the pool lights in the low-light photos is water vapor at near-saturation near the surface — telltale of either RH drifting above 60% or cold surfaces (skylights / monitor glazing) chilling the air column. Both are common in older natatoriums running overhead mixing ventilation.
  • Ductwork corrosion risk. The exposed galvanized spiral duct in this space is unprotected. In chloramine atmospheres the zinc coating blooms (white powder) and eventually the steel pits. If the building is more than ~15 years old this duct will show it on close inspection.

Uncertainty & Assumptions

  • Lane width. I assumed 7 ft. If the pool is built to 8 ft lanes (rarer but it happens in some rec builds), water width would be 48 ft and volume would rise to ~143,000 gal. The photos favor 7 ft because the deck-to-water proportions look tight.
  • Break point location. I placed it 10 ft off the deep wall. Common alternatives are 8 ft or 12 ft. This barely moves the volume — total stays within 123,000–126,000 gal across that range.
  • Deck widths. Side deck ~8 ft, deep end ~13 ft, shallow end ~7 ft are my reads of the photos. These drive the room-envelope number. The shallow-end deck could be as narrow as 5 ft or as wide as 10 ft depending on an equipment storage cutout I can't see clearly.
  • Ceiling height. Eave 14 ft and ridge 22 ft are estimates from joist-count scaling. These could each be off ±2 ft without changing the pool analysis at all; they matter only for air-volume and psychrometric calcs.
  • Number of lanes. The most certain number — six starting blocks visible in a full-width shot.
