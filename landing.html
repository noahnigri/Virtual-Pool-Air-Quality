Section 01 — The Problem

Indoor pools have an air-quality problem.

Chloramines — the byproducts of chlorine reacting with sweat, urine, and skin oils — off-gas into the air above indoor pools. Above safe thresholds they cause coughing, eye irritation, and asthma exacerbation. The most vulnerable are the ones most exposed: children in swim lessons, competitive swimmers logging thousands of hours, and lifeguards who breathe pool air all day.

Facility managers know the risk, but the tools that let them predict and manage it are complex CFD software licensed only to consulting firms. A pool manager can't run EnergyPlus on their laptop to answer "if I raise my outdoor-air fraction from 25% to 40%, how much better does the air get?"

That's the gap this project fills.

Section 02 — The Tool

Configure your facility. See what to change.

The IAQ Simulator packages 15+ peer-reviewed papers into a physics-based calculator that anyone can use. It replaces guesswork with a defensible number, and every prediction comes with an honest uncertainty band.

  • First-principles mass balance — steady-state indoor-air model using the Proulx & Hallé 2022 measured emission rate
  • Per-input uncertainty propagation — every parameter has a 1σ tier (Measured / Estimated / Default); the output band responds accordingly
  • Live code-compliance flags — CDC MAHC + Manual SPS + ASHRAE 62.1 thresholds checked in real time
  • Facility-type presets — rec pool, 50m competition, hotel, therapy, high-school natatorium — one click loads plausible parameters
  • Vulnerable population weighting — competitive swimmers, children, asthmatics correctly emit more precursor AND respond more to exposure

Section 03 — The Research

Every number in the model has a paper behind it.

The tool draws on 15+ peer-reviewed sources. Five of them do most of the work — they anchor the emission rate, the pH chemistry, the vulnerability multipliers, and the health-outcome thresholds.

Trichloramine exposure at indoor swimming pools: a critical review

Emmett · 2020

Consensus review of typical field measurements. The calculator's defaults are calibrated so a rec-pool configuration produces the 0.15–0.30 mg/m³ room-average band Emmett identifies as typical.

Read →

Lung hyperpermeability and asthma prevalence in schoolchildren

Bernard et al. · OEM · 2003

Landmark pediatric cohort (n=1,881) establishing the dose-response between cumulative pool attendance and childhood asthma. Source of the ×2.5 vulnerability multiplier for children under 12.

Read →

Childhood asthma and environmental exposures at swimming pools

Weisel et al. · EHP · 2009

Consensus review of the natatorium chloramine literature. Anchor for the tool's shower-compliance saturation curve and the combined-chlorine → NCl₃ relationship.

Read →

Numerical modeling of indoor air quality in an indoor swimming pool facility

Proulx & Hallé · Frontiers · 2022

CFD study providing the tool's per-m² emission rate (4×10⁻⁹ kg/s/m²) and the stratification base of 2.0× that maps room average to swimmer-zone concentration.

Read →

Occupational exposure to trichloramine and respiratory symptoms among indoor swimming pool workers

Fantuzzi et al. · Env. Intl. · 2013

Personal-air-sampling study of 128 pool workers across 20 facilities. Odds ratios of 2.9–3.6 for eye and respiratory irritation above 0.5 mg/m³ — the source of the WARNING-tier boundary.

Section 05 — Aligned With the IAQ Mission

Backed by the same principles as the IAQ Mission.

This project is built in alignment with the IAQ Mission, an industry-wide initiative championed by Olympic gold medalist Rowdy Gaines and Dehumidified Air Solutions. The mission advocates for source-capture ventilation, purpose-built pool dehumidification, and measurement discipline as the path forward for safer indoor pool air.

The interventions this tool ranks — raise outdoor-air fraction, install UV-C secondary disinfection, deploy overnight covers, control combined chlorine — are the same interventions the IAQ Mission advances at the policy and industry level. This tool gives individual facility operators a way to quantify what the mission argues qualitatively.

Section 06 — About the Project

Why I built this.

This is a high-school independent research project. I built it because my swim team practices at a pool where the air quality varied dramatically from one day to the next — some days you could barely finish a set without your eyes stinging — and there was no way to know why.

What started as a question — "why does the air feel worse on some days?" — turned into 18 months of learning atmospheric chemistry, indoor-air-quality physics, and how to package that rigor into something a facility manager can actually use on a Tuesday morning.

My mentor on the mechanical-engineering side is Raj Setty. Every threshold in the tool traces to a published source, and every prediction comes with an honest ±σ band. Feedback is welcome — I read every message.
